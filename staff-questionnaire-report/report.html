Internal report · Momentum Fitness · September 2026

Momentum Staff Voices

Every member of the team filled out the staff questionnaire — the CEO, the manager wearing three hats, the front desk, and all six trainers. This is what they said, what it means, and what we should build next.

9 responses 4 role tracks Aug 19–29, 2026 collection window 187 answers analyzed
The short version

Six things the questionnaire makes undeniable

  1. Client data lives in nine different pockets. Every trainer keeps client history in their own system — iPhone Notes, paper folders, email threads, even ChatGPT. Nothing is shared, and every trainer independently asked for the same fix: one member profile anyone can pull up.
  2. Billing is the one thing members actively complain about that we control. Members report surprise charges; the front desk can't answer billing questions; Josie traces most errors to one manual autopay process. The whole chain is fixable.
  3. Chase is the bottleneck, and he knows it. His own words: he shouldn't be doing 80% of his weekly list. He wants three jobs — vision, culture, finances — and is instead doing seventeen, from sales calls to taking out the trash.
  4. The staff is asking for consistency, not more ideas. Josie calls it "shiny object syndrome"; a trainer independently named "changing things that currently work just fine too often" as the single most broken thing. Execution debt is real.
  5. The culture is the moat — protect it. All nine responses, without exception, named the community, the training framework, or the people as the thing that must not change. Nobody said pricing, equipment, or the space.
  6. The February move is a client-retention project that hasn't started. Trainer estimates of how many clients survive the move range from "all of them" to "starting almost from scratch." Nobody is tracking it in one place.

Part 2 · Findings

What the answers actually say

2.1 · Client data is fragmented nine ways

Asked where client history lives — measurements, progress, session notes — the training staff described seven different personal systems with zero overlap. Typeset is the only shared tool (all six trainers plus Josie use it to build plans), but it's a delivery tool, not a record. The record itself is wherever each trainer happens to keep it.

2.2 · Billing is the top controllable member complaint

Member complaints cluster into two groups: things that are hard to change (a Texas gym runs warm) and one thing that is entirely ours — billing. The chain is visible across three independent responses: members get "charged without their knowledge" (trainer), the front desk's only stuck-point is "the billing situation" (Rylee), and the admin who cleans it up traces "the majority" of errors to one person's manual autopay process, with the rest from trainers scheduling at the wrong rate (Josie). Chase names billing fires as recurring fire #1.

2.3 · The discovery hand-off works on paper, not in practice

Trainer preparedness for session one after a discovery meeting averages 3.5/5 with a wide spread — one trainer feels fully briefed, another feels close to starting from scratch and mostly wishes he "got more handoffs" at all. The specific gaps trainers named are consistent and concrete:

  • Strength baseline — screenings capture limitations and range of motion, but nothing about actual strength, so session one is "a guessing game" (Josie).
  • Nutrition background — the functional screen is great; the nutrition picture arrives empty (David).
  • Medications — the med list is "sometimes not complete" (trainer).
  • Personality and confidence — how the client feels about starting, which shapes coaching from minute one.

2.4 · Chase is doing seventeen jobs and wants three

Chase's own weekly list: sales calls, discovery meetings, market research, role clarity, team meetings, team communications, implementing new systems, updating client nutrition plans, updating client training plans, client communications and check-ins, marketing tracking, networking, financials, strategic partnerships, cleaning the gym, racking weights, taking out the trash.

"I know I shouldn't be doing 80% of this. My role should be: 1. Create the vision, 2. Build the culture, 3. Manage the finances."— Chase

Asked what takes far more time than it deserves, he named exactly the things trainers also want automated or owned elsewhere: updating client nutrition and training plans, calling dead-end leads, and refereeing role clarity. Asked what he'd do with ten reclaimed hours a week: work out and spend time with his daughter. And asked what he wishes he could see every Monday morning, he wrote out an eighteen-metric CFO dashboard from memory — new members, CAC, LTV, MRR, ARR, churn, NRR, burn multiple, margins, CAC payback, cash runway. He is running the company's finances with no instrument panel.

One more answer deserves visibility rather than a bullet point. Asked what nobody has asked him about, Chase wrote that entrepreneurship is lonely, that no one checks on him, and that he carries the pressure of success in isolation. Whatever else this report changes, the operating partner reading it should know that.

2.5 · Physics 101: everyone agrees on the diagnosis

The group-class model is loved and its execution is not. Josie is blunt: five rotating coaches make it inconsistent, programming goes out the night before instead of four weeks ahead, and she'd hand the whole class to one or two dedicated owners. The trainers, asked independently, drew the same line she did: the program, timing, and cues should be identical in every class; personality is the only thing that should vary. David (a Physics coach) agrees the exercises must stay as written, with trainer judgment reserved for progressions and regressions. This is rare — a staffing change where the people affected already agree on the principle.

"It's so inconsistent right now with 5 coaches, & that's my least favorite thing about it… Overall, it's a great model. But I think it's being ruined by poor coach quality, a lack of effective exercise instruction, & disorganization."— Josie

2.6 · The noon–5 dead zone: staff see a demographic problem, not a programming problem

Nobody thinks the current membership will fill the afternoon — the people who train here work during it. Most ideas point at new daytime audiences; the practical blocker raised is that current trainers with families can't staff those hours.

2.9 · The front desk wants a bigger job

Rylee's questionnaire is an application to grow, hiding in plain sight. Her busiest work is facility upkeep; her stuck-point is billing questions; she rates her comfort answering the main phone at 4/5 with a cheat-sheet and backup; and asked what she'd most enjoy taking on, she picked "helping members with accounts and billing questions" — the exact thing members are confused about and Josie is buried under. Her one ask: training, plus someone to back her up if she gets stuck mid-call.

2.10 · Change fatigue is the quiet warning in the data

Two people, independently, named the pace of half-finished initiatives — not any specific problem — as the most broken thing at Momentum:

"Every week there's some new idea being brought up when the previous one wasn't even executed successfully. It's like shiny object syndrome coming from Chase's brain sometimes."— Josie
"Changing things that currently work just fine too often."— Trainer, on the single most broken thing

Josie adds the operational consequence: tasks get assigned to trainers without compensation attached, so they quietly don't get done, and procedures change before the last change settled. This finding should discipline everything in Part 5 — fewer initiatives, finished properly, beats more initiatives. It's also why the app list below is ranked: build one thing at a time.

2.11 · What everyone loves

Asked what must never change, all nine responses converge on the same three things: the community ("we know everyone by name," "the Cheers feeling," "we are a tribe"), the training framework ("no one else does it like we do"), and the people. One trainer added a fourth worth honoring: scheduling freedom — "having the choice to do what you want and when you want is one of the best things about Momentum." Any fix that erodes those four things costs more than it saves.

Part 3 · The balance sheet

Efficiencies & deficiencies

Working well — build on these

  • Client care instincts. Every trainer notices a quiet client fast and reaches out personally — one literally drives to their house. Retention culture exists; it just has no tooling.
  • Typeset adoption. One shared plan-building tool across all seven training staff — a rare piece of de facto standardization to anchor on.
  • Daily financial discipline. Payroll reconciliation happens every day without fail, with a double-check. Costly, but the habit is the hard part; automation is the easy part.
  • GHL lead capture. Chase confirms it intercepts and buckets leads well; the failure is downstream nurture, not capture.
  • The discovery meeting itself. Screening (FMS, now VALD, Fit3D) is thorough on movement; nobody wants it gone, just extended.
  • An eager front desk. Rylee is asking for more responsibility — pre-trained enthusiasm is the cheapest capacity you'll ever add.

Leaking time or trust — fix these

  • No shared client record. Nine pocket systems; shared clients suffer; a trainer departure would take client history with it.
  • Manual billing chain. One person's manual autopay causes most errors; members find out via surprise charges; the desk can't answer for it.
  • Founder as ops layer. 30 min/day reconciliation, plan updates, dead-lead calls — all sitting on the CEO.
  • Check-ins by hand. 25–30 unpaid staff-hours/week, much of it repetitive check-in and program-update work trainers want automated.
  • Night-before Physics programming. Should be a four-week block; currently a daily scramble across five coaches.
  • No instrument panel. Chase wants 18 metrics; Josie wants 3 reports; today both ask humans and wait.
  • Verbal membership changes. Members pause/change at the desk verbally; Josie needs it documented — disputes are inevitable.
  • Initiative churn. New systems launched before the last one landed; assignments without compensation quietly die.
Part 4 · Boundaries

What can change, what can't, what shouldn't

Fair game — change freely

  • The billing pipeline: kill the manual autopay, add reconciliation checks, publish a front-desk billing cheat-sheet.
  • The hand-off packet: add strength baseline, nutrition intake, med list, and a personality note to the DM output.
  • Physics 101 staffing: 1–2 dedicated owners, programming published 4 weeks ahead. Staff already agree.
  • Check-in and program-update workflow: automate the sending and chasing, keep the trainer's voice in the reply.
  • Reporting: a Monday dashboard for Chase, three auto-reports for Josie.
  • Legacy grandfathered rates (~10% of members): Chase explicitly wants the cleanup; sequence it after billing trust is repaired, not before.
  • Membership-change intake: a simple documented form replaces "mention it at the desk."
  • The front desk role: train Rylee up on billing and phones with a backup escalation path.
  • Meeting cadence: David wants weekly team meetings back; the old 1.5-hour Tuesday format was resented. Shorter, standing, weekly.

Constrained — can't change (for now)

  • The move date and distance. ~Feb 1, ~15 minutes away. Commute math is the top at-risk reason; mitigate, can't eliminate.
  • The current space until then — Chase's corporate-wellness play explicitly waits on the new building.
  • Mindbody's architecture. Records can be deactivated, never deleted; the clutter is permanent. Work around it (or plan a post-move platform decision), don't fight it.
  • Trainer afternoon availability. Family obligations are structural; dead-zone strategy must assume new hires or new audiences, not existing staff.
  • The 55/45 split, near-term. The consultant wants 45%; Chase judges that trainers would walk without a price rise to offset. Park it.
  • Texas heat. Mitigate with the new HVAC; it will never be a cold gym in August.
  • External pressures. The building purchase timeline and a pending legal claim from a former employee sit on Chase's mind; neither is solvable from inside operations, but both argue for taking load off him now.

Protected — could change, must not

  • The community and culture — named by all nine respondents. Every automation below should remove admin, never human contact.
  • The training framework and standard of care — "no one else does it like we do." Codify it, don't dilute it.
  • Trainer scheduling freedom — explicitly named as a reason people stay. Dead-zone staffing must not become mandatory afternoon shifts.
  • The staff itself — Josie: "aside from a couple, amazing." The bar for new hires is the current team, and everyone knows it.
Part 5 · Implementation

A sequence, not a wishlist

Finding 2.10 is the constraint: the team is tired of launches. So this is deliberately one workstream at a time, each finished and visibly working before the next starts.

Days 1–30
  • Stop the billing bleed. Move every manual autopay into Mindbody's native autopay; until done, a second person verifies each manual charge. Write Rylee's billing cheat-sheet (top 10 questions + when to escalate) and train her on it — she asked.
  • Start the move risk register. One shared list: every client, their trainer, move-risk level, and the retention play. The "15 of 19" trainer has already done his — copy his homework.
  • Membership-change form. One QR code at the desk; changes only count when submitted. Ends the verbal-change dispute cycle.
Days 31–60
  • Upgrade the DM hand-off. Add strength baseline, nutrition intake, and med-list confirmation to the discovery flow; output is a one-page brief the receiving trainer gets before session one. Target: no trainer below 4/5 on preparedness.
  • Physics 101 reset. Name 1–2 owners, compensate the role explicitly (finding 2.10: assignments without compensation die), publish blocks 4 weeks ahead.
  • Monday numbers, v1. Even manual at first: new members, revenue, churn, account balances, membership classifications — emailed every Monday. Chase stops asking humans for numbers.
Days 61–90
  • Build app #1 (Member Profile Hub, below) and migrate two volunteer trainers' books into it. Prove it before asking all six.
  • Automate check-ins through GHL for the volunteers' clients — trainers review and personalize replies instead of initiating every thread.
  • Pilot one dead-zone audience. David's seniors concept is the cheapest test: his clientele, midday hours, rehab-forward framework. One class, twice a week, measured.

Deliberately excluded from the first 90 days: legacy-rate cleanup (do it once billing errors are at zero, so the price conversation isn't happening on top of broken trust), the 55/45 split (parked), and any new marketing agency decisions.

Part 6 · Build list

Apps worth building, in order

Ranked by demand in the data × operational payoff. The first three each answer a problem that at least three respondents raised independently. All of them fit the pattern we already run — self-contained tools like the discovery email generator, integrated with GHL and Mindbody where their APIs allow.

1

Member Profile Hub

One profile per member: goals, restrictions, medications, screening results (FMS/VALD/Fit3D links), program history with weights and reps, check-in log, nutrition targets, session notes. Any trainer pulls it up in seconds; shared clients stop being a relay race.

Why first: every trainer asked for it unprompted and listed the same fields — the strongest mandate in the questionnaire. It also de-risks trainer departures and the move itself.

Demand: 7 of 7 training staffReplaces: phone notes, paper folders, email, ChatGPTKeys to get right: faster than the notes app, or it loses to the notes app
2

Check-in Autopilot & Retention Radar

Auto-sends weekly check-ins (the Google Form Josie already uses, standardized), chases non-responders, and summarizes replies per client. The radar side watches Mindbody attendance: quiet client + missed sessions = a flag on the trainer's list while the save is still cheap. Trainers keep the personal outreach — the app just tells them who and when.

Why second: converts the 25–30 unpaid weekly staff-hours into coaching time, and turns the team's excellent retention instincts into a system that never misses.

Demand: named by 4 respondentsFeeds: Profile Hub check-in logRails: GHL for messaging, Mindbody for attendance
3

Billing Guard

A daily automatic three-way reconciliation — Mindbody appointments vs. services vs. Worthington deposits — that flags mismatches instead of waiting for Chase to find them. Front-desk mode gives Rylee a searchable billing FAQ and a one-tap "escalate to Josie with context" button.

Why third: erases Chase's 30 minutes a day and Tiffany's double-check, catches the mischarges before members do, and unblocks the legacy-rate cleanup Chase wants.

Demand: Chase's fire #1, Rylee's stuck-point, Josie's cleanup burdenWins back: ~3.5 founder-hours/week + member trust
4

Monday Dashboard

Chase's eighteen metrics, built honestly: the five that matter weekly (new members, MRR, churn, revenue, account balances) live and automatic; the investor-grade set (CAC, LTV, NRR, burn multiple, runway) computed monthly. Josie's three reports — balances, revenue, membership classifications — are the same data, so she gets hers free.

Demand: Chase (18 metrics), Josie (3 reports)Note: start with what Mindbody/GHL exports can actually feed; no vanity numbers
5

Discovery Hand-off Builder

A guided DM intake that captures what trainers said is missing — strength baseline, nutrition background, complete med list, personality/confidence notes — and generates the one-page brief automatically into the Profile Hub. The discovery email generator we already built is the natural front half of this flow.

Demand: 4 trainers + Josie named specific gapsTarget: preparedness floor moves from 2/5 to 4/5
6

Physics 101 Program Board

Four-week block calendar: the owner publishes blocks ahead, every coach sees the same exercises, timing, and cues for each class, and substitutions get logged with a reason. Makes "identical program, personal delivery" — the standard every trainer independently endorsed — enforceable instead of aspirational.

Demand: Josie + every Physics-adjacent trainerDepends on: the staffing decision, not the other way around
7

Move Mission Control

The risk register, productized for the five months that matter: every client's move-risk status, assigned retention play, outreach log, and a weekly keep-rate projection per trainer and for the gym. Sunsets itself in March. If only one thing gets built before February, the 30-day manual version of this is it.

Demand: implicit in 2.7 — nobody owns this todayDeadline-bound: value drops to zero after the move
8

Content Pipeline Board

Josie's bottlenecks are editing time and trainer availability. A lightweight board — content ideas ranked by past performance, a monthly batch-filming day with trainer sign-ups, and a queue showing what's filmed/edited/scheduled — attacks both. Also the diplomatic channel for the note that raw, unpolished Chase outperforms scripted Chase.

Demand: Josie's whole social hatCheapest win: the batch-filming calendar needs no code at all

Sequencing note: apps 1, 2 and 5 share one data model (the member profile). Build the Hub first and the other two become features of it rather than separate tools — one login, one system to learn, which is exactly what a change-fatigued team needs.
